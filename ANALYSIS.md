# Connecting a dependable commute with a safer campus trip

The survey suggests that campus travel often works well, while the journey to campus varies with location, time, and personal circumstances. The improvement story is to connect the whole trip: reaching campus, moving safely within it, and getting home when needed.

## Descriptive evidence

The default analysis includes 810 U.S.-eligible responses among 816 records. Responses span February 21 to April 7, 2025.

## Six exploratory themes

### The commute can be harder than the campus trip

Respondents distinguish campus walkability and connectivity from the residential approaches needed to reach it. “If anything, the issue lies with transportation *to* campus and not on it.” (Network question, workbook row 776.) Another describes a convenient bus trip becoming impractical after moving farther away, with an hour-long trip and multiple transfers (row 297).

This suggests examining complete door-to-door journeys and off-campus approaches. Convenient core service cannot establish that all commute needs are met.

### Transit is valued, but trust depends on time and coverage

Praise for MTD coexists with specific operational difficulties. “Overall good. Bus network is strong but should have better off-peak frequencies.” (Network question, row 84.) Other accounts describe crowding, early or late arrivals, inconvenient hours, and indirect trips.

These accounts align with the closed-question barriers on time, frequency, and reliability. A useful investigation would identify where and when transit becomes difficult to depend on, rather than assigning the network a single satisfaction score.

### Sharing space creates uncertainty about who goes where

Some respondents emphasize cars and ask for protected routes and calmer streets. Others emphasize cyclists, pedestrians, and device users who disregard rules. One asks for physical separation to reduce vehicle intrusion and dooring (Safety question, row 230); another asks for cyclist and scooter enforcement (row 201).

These are competing explanations of risk. Clearer design, education, and consistent enforcement can be considered together. The comments do not establish which mode is responsible for observed incidents.

### Safety changes with darkness, weather, and route condition

Some routes feel acceptable by day but uncomfortable after dark. Snow clearance and poor surfaces also change route usability. “Bike lanes and paths are the last areas to be cleared and sometimes are not cleared before the snow melts.” (Safety question, row 332.)

Lighting, maintenance, winter clearance, and evening connections belong to the usable network. Overall safety ratings can obscure these conditional experiences.

### Changing modes must fit responsibilities beyond campus

Driving supports caregiving, work tasks, distance, and emergencies for some respondents. “However for some folks - single parents with children - a car is the only reasonable option to get to campus.” (Network question, row 327.) Another praises buses but needs a car to reach a child's school (row 417).

Requests to reduce car traffic coexist with requests for affordable and flexible parking. This suggests evaluating dependable alternatives and practical flexibility alongside incentives, without assuming that every drive-alone trip is equally substitutable.

### A route is only accessible when every link works

Comments describe poor ramps, long detours to accessible entrances, blocked sidewalks, and nonfunctioning audio signals. “I am visually impaired and oftentimes the audio walk signals do not function.” (Safety question, row 601.) Another describes assigned parking far from a usable accessible entrance (row 482).

A small number of disability-related comments does not imply a low priority. Complete-route review with affected users can uncover barriers hidden by average ratings.

## Implications and limits

Possible investigations include residential approaches, bus operations by corridor and time, crossing and bike-route continuity, nighttime journeys, winter maintenance, flexible parking, and complete accessible routes. These are questions suggested by the survey, not funded actions or proven remedies.

The two open-ended prompts provide 914 written responses from 563 distinct source rows. Fifty-two selected full responses were read in context and coded by AI; 862 received provisional automated suggestions. The six themes are an exploratory synthesis of selected and contrasting accounts linked to descriptive evidence. Exact quoted excerpts were checked against the workbook.

This is not a fully human-validated corpus analysis. Code frequencies remain provisional and may reflect praise, criticism, or neutral mentions. No sentiment score, saturation claim, causal conclusion, or intercoder reliability claim is made. The app provides all response-level assignments, definitions, rules, rationales, and an editing workflow for contextual review.
